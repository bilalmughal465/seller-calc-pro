eBay Seller Mistakes: 7 Costly Mistakes New Sellers Make

Short answer: a $100 sale in a standard eBay category costs $14.00 in fees — a 13.6% final value fee ($13.60) plus the $0.40 per-order fee — before shipping, and 7 common mistakes add more on top: pricing the fee on the item price only, forgetting the per-order fee, listing past the 250 free listings, Promoted Listings ad fees on the whole sale, believing "no returns" protects you, slipping to Below Standard, and the international fee. Work the real number in the eBay Final Value Fee Calculator before you list.

Stand: November 2026. Sources: eBay's selling-fees documentation as reported by independent fee trackers (final value fee 13.6% in most categories, per-order fee $0.40 above $10.00 / $0.30 at or below $10.00, first 250 listings free then $0.35 each, international fee 1.65%), re-checked 28 November 2026; eBay's own $100-sale maths from SellerCalc's eBay $100-sale guide. Rates change — check eBay's selling-fees page for your category before you price. No sales-tax or income-tax advice is given here.

The formula

Fee base = item price + shipping the buyer pays + sales tax

Final value fee = 13.6% × fee base (most categories, no Store or Starter Store; 2.35% on the portion above $7,500)

Per-order fee = $0.40 on orders over $10.00 ($0.30 on orders of $10.00 or less)

Profit = fee base − final value fee − per-order fee − label you pay − item cost − packing − any ad fee

The whole guide hangs on the first line: eBay charges its percentage on the total amount of the sale, not on the item price. Shipping the buyer pays is fee-bearing revenue on one side and a cost you pay to the carrier on the other — it is counted twice in your maths if you forget either side, and the fee on it is the gap that turns a thin profit into a loss. Worked example 1: the $40 item that looked profitable

A $40 item, $30 item cost, buyer pays $10 shipping, your label costs $12, standard category, no Store.

Fee base. $40.00 item + $10.00 buyer-paid shipping = $50.00.

Final value fee. 13.6% × $50.00 = $6.80.

Per-order fee. Order over $10.00, so $0.40. Total eBay fees: $6.80 + $0.40 = $7.20.

Profit. $50.00 revenue − $7.20 fees − $12.00 label − $30.00 item cost = $0.80 profit.

If you had priced the fee on the item price only — 13.6% × $40.00 = $5.44 + $0.40 = $5.84 — you would have expected $2.16 profit. The $1.36 fee on the shipping is the whole difference, and it is more than the profit you actually made. This is the failure mode in Margeo's worked example too: their $40 item with a $30 cost and $8 of shipping paid comes out at a $3.84 loss once the fee base is counted correctly.

Worked example 2: a $100 sale with buyer-paid shipping

A $100 item, buyer pays $10 shipping, your label costs $10, item cost $60, standard category, no Store, no ad fee.

Fee base. $100.00 + $10.00 = $110.00.

Final value fee. 13.6% × $110.00 = $14.96. Per-order fee $0.40. Total fees $15.36.

You keep before costs. $110.00 − $15.36 = $94.64.

Profit. $94.64 − $10.00 label − $60.00 item cost = $24.64 profit.

Compare that with the same $100 item and free shipping: the fee base is $100.00, the fee is $14.00, you keep $86.00, and the $10.00 label still comes out of it — $86.00 − $10.00 − $60.00 = $16.00 profit. Charging the buyer shipping is worth $8.64 of profit here, because the $10.00 of shipping revenue costs only $1.36 of extra fee. Free shipping is a pricing decision, not a fee saving — the fee follows the total either way. See how much eBay takes from a $100 sale for the no-shipping version of this table.

The 7 mistakes

1. Pricing the fee on the item price only. The fee base is item price + buyer-paid shipping + sales tax. On a $50 item with $10 shipping, the fee is 13.6% of $60 ($8.16), not of $50 ($6.80) — a $1.36 gap, more than a third of a typical margin on a low-cost item. Fix: always add the shipping you charge into the fee base before you multiply. The eBay Final Value Fee Calculator does this for you.

2. Forgetting the $0.40 per-order fee. It is small in dollars and large in percentage: $0.40 is 1% of a $40 sale and 4% of a $10 sale. On a $10 order the per-order fee is $0.30, and the final value fee is $1.36, so the fixed fee is nearly a quarter of the total fee. Cheap items sold one at a time are where this bites hardest — bundle them or raise the price. Fix: add the per-order fee to every sale's maths, not just the big ones.

3. Listing past the 250 free listings. The first 250 listings each month have no insertion fee; after that, the standard insertion fee is $0.35 per listing. One hundred extra listings a month is $35.00 of insertion fees before anything sells — and relisting the same unsold item counts as a new listing. Fix: count your live listings against the 250, end stale listings instead of relisting them, and check whether a Store subscription's extra free listings cost less than the insertion fees you are paying. See how to calculate shipping cost for eBay for the other half of the listing maths.

4. Paying a Promoted Listings ad fee on the whole sale. The ad fee is a percentage of the total sale — item price plus shipping plus tax — not of the item price alone, and it is charged only when the promoted listing sells. At a 10% ad rate on a $110 total, the ad fee is $11.00, not $10.00, and it sits on top of the $15.36 of standard fees in worked example 2, taking the total to $26.36. Fix: set the ad rate from the total, not the item price, and read eBay Promoted Listings mistakes before you promote a listing. Work the ad fee in the Promoted Listings Fee Calculator.

5. Believing "no returns" protects you. A no-returns policy refuses returns for buyer's remorse only. If an item arrives not as described, damaged, or not at all, the buyer can open a case under eBay's Money Back Guarantee and eBay can force the return anyway — and the return shipping is usually yours. Worse, listings that accept returns rank better in search, so "no returns" costs visibility as well as protection. Fix: accept returns with a clear window, photograph and describe honestly so "not as described" cases stay rare, and budget a small return rate into every price instead of assuming zero returns.

6. Slipping to Below Standard. eBay adds an extra 6% final value fee on applicable sales while an account is Below Standard — so a 13.6% category becomes 19.6% — and the account loses search visibility and Top Rated Plus benefits at the same time. On a $100 sale that is $6.00 of extra fee, every sale, until the level recovers. Late shipments, defects and cases are what push an account down. Fix: set handling time you can actually meet, ship with tracking, and answer cases fast — the level is protected by operations, not by pricing.

7. Missing the international fee. When the buyer or the delivery address is outside your registered country, eBay adds an extra international fee of 1.65% of the total sale — $1.65 on a $100 international sale, $16.50 on a $1,000 one — unless you ship through eBay International Shipping, which waives it. It is small per sale and real across a month of cross-border orders, and it is the fee most new sellers have never heard of. Fix: check the buyer's country before you celebrate an international sale, and use eBay International Shipping where it fits.

Frequently asked questions

What is eBay's final value fee?

In most categories on eBay.com, the final value fee is 13.6% of the total amount of the sale for sellers without a Store or with a Starter Store, plus a per-order fee of $0.40 on orders over $10.00 ($0.30 on orders of $10.00 or less). The rate varies by category and Store subscription, and it is 2.35% on the portion of a sale above $7,500 per item. Check eBay's selling-fees page for your category before you price.

Does eBay charge fees on shipping?

Yes. eBay charges the final value fee on the total amount of the sale, which generally includes the item price, handling charges, shipping collected from the buyer, and sales tax. On a $40 item with $10 of buyer-paid shipping, the fee base is $50, not $40 — the extra $1.36 of fee is the single most common beginner mistake.

How many free listings does eBay give each month?

For many casual sellers on eBay.com, the first 250 listings each month have no insertion fee. After that allowance, the standard insertion fee is $0.35 per listing, with category exclusions and different allowances for some Store subscriptions. One hundred extra listings a month costs $35.00 in insertion fees alone.

Does saying no returns protect an eBay seller?

No. A no-returns policy does not stop eBay's Money Back Guarantee: if an item arrives not as described, damaged, or not at all, the buyer can still open a case and eBay can force the return. A no-returns policy only refuses returns for buyer's remorse, and even then it costs search visibility, because eBay favours listings that accept returns. Budget for a small return rate instead of assuming zero returns.

What happens if an eBay seller drops to Below Standard?

eBay adds an extra 6% final value fee on applicable sales while the account is Below Standard, on top of the standard rate — so a 13.6% category becomes 19.6%. The account also loses search visibility and Top Rated Plus benefits. Late shipments, defects and cases are what push an account down, so handling time and tracking matter as much as price.

Does eBay charge an international fee?

Yes. When the buyer or the delivery address is outside your registered country, eBay adds an extra international fee of 1.65% of the total sale, unless you ship through eBay International Shipping, which waives it. On a $100 international sale that is $1.65 on top of the standard fee — small per sale, real across a month of cross-border orders.
