How to Calculate Dimensional Weight (DIM Weight): Formula, Divisors and Worked Examples

Short answer: dimensional weight is length × width × height in inches, divided by the carrier divisor — 139 for UPS and FedEx daily rates, 166 for retail rates — rounded up to the next whole pound. The carrier bills on whichever is greater, the dimensional weight or the actual weight. An 18 in × 14 in × 12 in box holding a 5 lb item bills at 22 lb on a 139 divisor, so you pay for 17 lb of air. The formula

DIM weight (lb) = length (in) × width (in) × height (in) ÷ divisor

Billable weight = whichever is greater: the DIM weight or the actual weight, both rounded up

Dimensional weight, also called volumetric weight or cubic weight, is a billing weight a carrier calculates from the size of your box instead of its mass, so it can charge for the space a light, bulky parcel takes up in the truck or plane. A parcel full of foam costs the carrier almost as much space as a parcel full of metal, and DIM weight is how that space gets priced. Four steps, in this order — the order matters, because the rounding rules are where the declared weight and the billed weight diverge.

Step 1 — Measure the outside of the packed box at its longest point on each axis, in inches. A carton that bulges is measured at the bulge. Round each dimension up to the next whole inch first — a box measured at 17.4 in is billed as 18 in.

Step 2 — Multiply length × width × height to get cubic inches.

Step 3 — Divide by the divisor: 139 on UPS and FedEx daily rates, 166 on retail rates and USPS.

Step 4 — Round up any fraction of a pound to the next whole pound. That is the dimensional weight. Compare it with the actual weight, also rounded up, and the carrier bills on the larger one.

The worked example: an 18 x 14 x 12 in box at 5 lb

This is the classic small-seller case — a light item in a box much bigger than it needs.

Volume. 18 × 14 × 12 = 3,024 cubic inches.

DIM weight at 139. 3,024 ÷ 139 = 21.76, rounded up to 22 lb.

DIM weight at 166. 3,024 ÷ 166 = 18.22, rounded up to 19 lb.

Billable weight. The actual weight is 5 lb, so the DIM weight wins on both divisors: 22 lb at 139, 19 lb at 166. At 139 you pay for 17 lb of air (22 − 5); at 166 you pay for 14 lb of air (19 − 5). The same box costs more under a 139 divisor than under 166, because a smaller divisor gives a higher DIM weight.

Cross-check: a 24 in × 18 in × 12 in carton is 5,184 cubic inches, and at 139 that is 37.3 lb, rounded up to 38 lb — the same figure the DIM-weight references publish for those inputs, so the arithmetic holds. The last row is the one sellers forget: DIM weight only bites when the box is light for its size. A small, dense box of 12 × 10 × 8 in has a DIM weight of 960 ÷ 139 = 6.91, rounded up to 7 lb, but the item actually weighs 10 lb, so the carrier bills on the 10 lb. The break-even rule is simple: the largest box volume you can ship before DIM overtakes the actual weight is the actual weight times the divisor — a 5 lb item can fill 5 × 139 = 695 cubic inches before DIM starts to bite.

How to shrink the charge

Use the smallest box the item fits in. Every extra inch of each side multiplies into the volume, so one inch on all three sides of an 18 × 14 × 12 in box adds 19 × 15 × 13 − 3,024 = 681 cubic inches — about 5 lb of DIM weight at 139. Measure the item, not the box you happen to have.

Remove excess void fill. Crumpled paper and bubble wrap fill space that you pay DIM weight for. Pack tight and use only the fill the item needs.

Vacuum-seal soft goods. Clothes, pillows and plush items compress to a fraction of their shipped volume in a vacuum bag, and the DIM weight falls with the box size.

Compare flat-rate boxes. USPS Priority Mail flat-rate boxes ignore dimensional weight entirely — if the item fits, the DIM weight is zero by definition. See the USPS vs UPS guide for when flat rate beats weight-based pricing.

Split the shipment only after checking the extra label. Two smaller, denser boxes can bill lower in total than one big light one, but each extra box adds a label and a base charge — run both options through the Shipping Cost Comparison Calculator before you split.

Three mistakes that cost sellers on DIM weight

Measuring the inside of the box. Carriers measure the outside, at the longest point on each axis, and they round each dimension up. Measuring the inside understates the volume and the bill surprises you — see the eBay shipping cost guide for the same measurement trap on marketplace sales.

Using the wrong divisor. 139 and 166 give very different answers on the same box — 22 lb against 19 lb on the worked example above. A calculator that defaults to one divisor while your carrier bills on the other quotes the wrong price on every sale. Check your rate sheet.

Forgetting the round-up. DIM weight rounds up twice: each dimension rounds up to the next whole inch, and the result rounds up to the next whole pound. 21.76 lb is billed as 22 lb, not 21 lb — and 17.4 in is billed as 18 in, not 17 in.

Method and limits

The formula is DIM weight = length × width × height ÷ divisor, with dimensions in inches and the result in pounds, and billable weight = the greater of the DIM weight and the actual weight, both rounded up — the same formula the SellerCalc Dimensional Weight Calculator runs. The divisors are 139 cubic inches per pound on UPS and FedEx daily (account) rates and 166 on retail rates, with 5,000 cm³/kg for international express and 6,000 cm³/kg for IATA air cargo; the USPS divisor is reported differently by different sources after a July 2026 change, so the USPS rate sheet is the authority for that number. Each dimension is rounded up to the next whole inch before the division, and the result is rounded up to the next whole pound. This guide covers the weight calculation only: it does not price the label, the service, fuel surcharges, residential delivery fees or insurance, and it does not cover oversize or overweight surcharges, which are separate charges on top of the billable weight. Treat the total as a planning estimate and confirm the final charge with your carrier before you quote a customer. Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate dimensional weight?

Measure the outside of the packed box in inches, rounding each dimension up to the next whole inch. Multiply length times width times height to get cubic inches, divide by the carrier divisor — 139 for UPS and FedEx daily rates, 166 for retail rates — and round the result up to the next whole pound. That is the dimensional weight; the carrier bills on whichever is greater, the dimensional weight or the actual weight.

What is the DIM divisor for UPS and FedEx?

The standard divisor is 139 cubic inches per pound on UPS and FedEx daily (account) rates, and 166 on retail and counter rates. A lower divisor means a higher dimensional weight for the same box, so 139 is the harsher of the two. Large shippers can negotiate a higher divisor in their contract — check your rate sheet for your number.

What is the DIM divisor for USPS?

USPS has used 166 cubic inches per pound on retail rates. Some sources report USPS moved to 139 in July 2026, while others still list 166, so the two disagree — check the current USPS rate sheet or price calculator for the divisor on your service before you quote a customer.

Do I pay the DIM weight or the actual weight?

You pay whichever is greater. The carrier compares the dimensional weight with the actual scale weight, both rounded up, and bills on the larger one. A light, bulky box bills on its dimensional weight; a small, dense box bills on its actual weight.

How can I reduce dimensional weight charges?

Use the smallest box the item fits in, because every extra inch of each side multiplies into the volume. Remove excess void fill, vacuum-seal soft goods like clothes, and compare flat-rate boxes, which ignore dimensional weight entirely. Splitting one big light box into two smaller denser ones can also help, but check the extra label cost first.

Does dimensional weight apply to every package?

It applies when the dimensional weight is higher than the actual weight, which is usually the case for light, bulky parcels. Small, dense parcels are normally billed on their actual weight because it is the greater of the two. Carriers apply dimensional weight on air services and on most ground services — check the rules for the service you are buying.
